# Reverse Outlining (Backwards Outlining)

Three related approaches to the same technique: outline the paper as it
actually exists after drafting, then compare that outline against what
the argument needs. Use whichever framing fits the draft at hand.

## A. Reverse Outlining Worksheet

A self-examination of argument and analysis according to a prompt.

### Basic Argument

- A. Write down your prompt (all important aspects of it).
- B. Write down your argument. What is your response or answer to the
  prompt? In other words, what are you trying to say?

### Examination — Introduction

- A. Briefly explain how the introduction is organized. Do you have a
  hook? Bridge? Where is your thesis?
- B. Thesis: write down your entire thesis.
- C. Now, very specifically explain how your thesis answers the prompt.
  In what way?

### Examination — Body Paragraphs (repeat for every body paragraph)

- A. Write down the main argument of the paragraph.
- B. Write down all types of evidence you are using (e.g., text,
  interview) and explain how each piece of evidence proves your point.
- C. Do you think your argument supports your thesis? Why or why not?

### Examination — Conclusion

- A. Explain in what way you summarize your point. Also, explain how
  your summary is equivalent to your thesis (remember, do not repeat
  your thesis!).
- B. Write down how your conclusion concluded. Do you stop at
  summarizing your points? Or are you going further and giving a more
  interesting perspective about your argument?
- C. In what way is your conclusion strong? In what way does it need
  improvement? What could you do differently?

*Source: Sam Park, Student Learning Center, University of California,
Berkeley. ©2008 UC Regents.*

## B. First-Draft Reverse Outlining Checklist

Revising is probably the second hardest part of the writing process,
only surpassed by actually starting to write. Reverse outlining
simplifies the task of revision by letting you see immediately where you
can improve your paper.

Note: the parts of an essay are called *points* and *elements* here
because *paragraph* is too constricting. All strong essays include
claims, evidence, and analysis, but do not necessarily divide them into
an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion.

Outline the finished essay by writing down the sentence(s) that
accomplish each of these points (or mark each part in a different
color):

**Introductory element:**
- Provides a brief but concise background on the text(s) you will be
  discussing or analyzing.
- Presents an observation, position, point of view, etc., that you are
  going to defend in the essay (thesis) — a conclusion that you came to,
  but others might not necessarily agree with.

**Each point:**
- Presents the point you will analyze/defend (commonly referred to as
  claims or topic sentences).
- (Optional, recommended, from the second point on) A transition: how
  and why this claim relates to your previous points.
- Evidence from the text(s) that illustrates why your claim is logical,
  reasonable, or true. Generally each claim requires two or more
  separate pieces of evidence to be convincing.
- Analysis of why the evidence supports the argument in your claim and
  how it relates to the thesis.

**Conclusion:**
- Sums up all of your points and thesis.
- Leaves readers feeling satisfied.

### Fixing what the outline exposes

- **Missing/unclear thesis statement:** Pinpoint exactly what you are
  trying to argue. Generally this is some debatable conclusion that you
  came to, one that other readers might not necessarily agree with.
- **Missing/unclear claims:** Ask yourself why your thesis is valid. The
  thesis makes a broader observation that you are going to defend, while
  the claims serve as subpoints where you analyze specific parts of the
  text.
- **Missing/unclear evidence:** Ask yourself what occurs in the text
  that made you make your claim. Sometimes it's easier to work in
  reverse: find a group of related evidence and make a claim from it.
- **Missing/unclear analysis:** Ask yourself why the claim you make and
  the evidence you choose are significant. What was particularly
  striking or resonant about the passages you chose to analyze? What is
  the overall significance of the evidence? Why did you choose that
  evidence to put in your paper?
- **Transitions:** Deciding how you order your points comes down to what
  you think is the logical order. Chronological analysis is usually a
  good start. Generally, your strongest claim, evidence, and analysis
  should come last, to conclude the essay on a strong point.
  Transitioning involves thinking about how your claims relate to each
  other and to the thesis as a whole.

## C. The Toolbox: Backwards Outline with "So What?"

Often a finished paper is "nearly there" but isn't held together tightly
enough: it makes all the right arguments and has the right points, but
they aren't clearly connected to the thesis. Think of the thesis or main
idea as a project, and the body paragraphs as the tools needed to
complete it. How the tools are used determines the final product.

**Construct a backwards outline** — an outline of the paper as it exists
now, after it's finished. Write a phrase or sentence that accurately
summarizes the main idea of each paragraph. Either the topic sentence,
the concluding sentence, or some combination of the two should give
precisely this information. (Never mind what you *want* to see as the
main idea — write down what's actually there.)

**Then interrogate each entry.** Ask: "What does each body paragraph do
to prove or help explain my thesis?" Does it complicate an assumption?
Does it exemplify a change? Does it explain the meaning of or reasoning
behind a certain element of the topic? Does it explain why a certain
concept is important in the greater context of the topic? In short: is
there a **"so what?"** for each paragraph?

If the answer is unclear, clarify. Explain specifically what the
examples and analysis in each body paragraph have to do with the central
claim. What do they prove/illuminate/explicate, and how does this
contribute to the general argument?

The ideas following the "so what?"s function as drafts of new topic or
concluding sentences for their respective paragraphs. They remind the
reader what the examples have to do with the original claim, rather than
just providing information and expecting the reader to sort it out and
make that extra step to the argument all alone. Each paragraph then
functions as a tool which not only makes a claim related to the thesis
but ALSO shows just how it is related and why it's important.
